Play-by-play study — does the end-of-quarter 2-for-1 pay?

A full-season play-by-play study in the basketball-data-science family: event data turned into a basketball decision.

The question. With 30–40 seconds left in a quarter, a team with the ball can shoot quickly — hoping to get one more possession before the buzzer, two shots for the opponent's one — or hold for the last shot and deny the opponent any reply. Coaches genuinely disagree. This measures both sides of the trade on a full season.

Terms used in this analysis. Dotted-underlined terms anywhere below repeat these definitions on hover (full glossary).

  • FGA — Field-goal attempts.
  • eFG% — Effective field-goal percentage: field-goal percentage with made threes counted 1.5x, putting twos and threes on one points scale.
  • 2-for-1 — Shooting early with 25-36 seconds left in a period so your team gets two possessions to the opponent's one before the buzzer.
  • CI — Confidence interval.

1. What play-by-play data is publicly available?

Source Content Public? Used
PlayByPlayV3 (stats.nba.com) Every logged event: period, game clock, action type, player, running score, shot x/y + distance Yes, live ✅ primary
PlayByPlayV2 Older schema, same events, no shot coordinates Yes, live ✗
ShotChartDetail ~102k shots/season with LOC_X/LOC_Y, zone labels, clock Yes, live ✗ (this study needs non-shot events too)
LeagueGameFinder Game index by season Yes, live ✅ (game IDs)
ESPN PBP via hoopR (R) Parallel feed, different event coding Yes ✗

All are reached through the nba_api Python package, which is an unofficial but well-maintained wrapper over the same public stats.nba.com endpoints the league's own site calls.

Coverage: the full 2023-24 regular season — 1,230 games, ~500 events each.

Worth noting for the tracking question: PlayByPlayV3 carries xLegacy/yLegacy court coordinates on shot events. That is genuine spatial data, live and current — unlike raw tracking, which is only available as a frozen 2015-16 archive (see tracking-study).

The reconciliation is not ceremonial. The Python implementation originally sorted events by actionNumber as text — "100" before "2" — which scrambled the score forward-fill and corrupted every window score. The bug was caught in review by re-deriving the table with a numeric sort; the independent R implementation now guards that class of error permanently, the same discipline as jersey-height-study.

One parquet per game, so an interrupted harvest resumes and the analysis never re-hits the network.

3. Method and every judgment call

Decision Value Why
Periods 1–3 only Q4 is contaminated by score-margin strategy, intentional fouling, and clock-stopping that have nothing to do with 2-for-1
Window final 60 seconds Wide enough to contain the whole decision, narrow enough that the rest of the quarter does not dilute it
Treatment first FGA at ≥ 32s Conventional threshold: enough for a 24-second opponent possession plus a final possession of your own
Outcome score delta, not summed shot values Free throws in this feed carry an empty shotResult, so summing shot values would silently drop every and-1 and shooting foul. Score deltas capture all scoring
Second outcome net points (own minus opponent, same window) Holding for the last shot is valuable because it denies the opponent a reply — an own-points outcome misses the entire cost side of the trade. Both outcomes are reported
Score handling forward-fill across the game Scores are only stamped on scoring events; the fill runs across the whole game, not within period, because periods start from the running total
Uncertainty bootstrap, not a t-test Points in a 60-second window are a small, lumpy, non-normal count (0, 2, 3, 5…)
Unit of analysis team-period One row per team per period, not per game

The confound this design cannot remove

A team can only run a 2-for-1 if it happens to have the ball early in the window. Which teams have the ball at 40 seconds is not random — they likely just got a stop, and may be attacking an unset defense.

This is stated up front rather than buried, a restricted-sample sensitivity analysis is reported alongside the headline, and the write-up does not claim a causal effect.

4. Limitations

  1. Observational, not causal. See above. The treatment is chosen by teams, not assigned.
  2. Possession is inferred from shots, not tracked — in the original design. Now addressed: Finding 4 redoes the analysis on real possession rows and quantifies exactly how often this proxy misread the choice (it flags 82% of true quick attempts, but only half of true holds).
  3. One season. 2023-24 only. No test of whether the pattern holds across eras or rule changes.
  4. No score-margin control. A team down 20 behaves differently from a tied team. Not conditioned on.
  5. eFG% ignores free throws. The shot-quality comparison uses field goals only, so it understates the value of drawing a shooting foul on a drive.
  6. Team quality is not controlled. Good offenses both score more and are more likely to have the ball, which inflates the raw gap.

Findings

Based on 1,230 games of 2023-24 play-by-play. Every number is generated by python/03_findings.py from output/, not typed by hand.

1. Teams really do hunt the 2-for-1

Of the 7,221 team-periods with a shot in the final 60 seconds of Q1–Q3, 78% took their first shot with 32 or more seconds left.

The share alone is weak evidence — later in the window there is simply more opportunity to shoot. The strong evidence is a local spike exactly at the threshold:

First shot at Mean team-periods per second
22–30s (the wait) 68
31–33s (the threshold) 201
36–40s (later, more opportunity) 147

Teams bunch at 31–33 seconds 1.37× more than at 36–40 seconds, even though the later band has more chances to shoot and should be higher if nothing special happened at 32. That discontinuity is the behaviour itself, visible in the raw counts rather than assumed: coaches are running this play deliberately. See figures/fig1_first_shot_timing.png.

2. The 2-for-1 is associated with more points — on both sides of the ball

First shot ≥ 32s (2-for-1) First shot < 32s (held)
Team-periods 5,614 1,607
Mean points in window 2.347 1.767
Mean opponent points 2.183 2.283
Mean net points +0.163 -0.516
Mean field-goal attempts 2.47 1.52
Effective FG% 0.506 0.436

Own-points difference: +0.579 per team-period (95% bootstrap CI [+0.475, +0.679], excludes zero, 4,000 resamples).

Why net matters: the case for holding the ball is precisely that it denies the opponent a reply, so an outcome that only counts a team's own points misses half the trade. On net points the raw gap is +0.679 (95% CI [+0.544, +0.816]) — but read the sensitivity analysis below before taking either raw gap at face value, because the "held" group includes many team-periods where the opponent controlled the window.

The mechanism is visible in the FGA row: roughly +0.94 extra shots per window. That is the extra possession, showing up exactly where it should.

Sensitivity: narrowing the selection gap

Restricting to team-periods whose first shot came between 20 and 45 seconds — so both groups had the ball at broadly similar times — the own-points gap is +0.364 (95% CI [+0.216, +0.512], excludes zero) and the net gap is +0.188 (95% CI [-0.002, +0.375], includes zero; n = 2,199 vs 842).

Both gaps shrink under the restriction, which is what you would expect if part of the headline gap is selection rather than strategy. The own-points advantage survives; the net advantage narrows to the edge of what one season of data can distinguish from zero. That asymmetry is itself informative: the extra possession is real, but the opponent's reply claws back much of its value.

3. The expected cost never appears

The standard objection to the 2-for-1 is that you rush a bad shot to create the extra possession. In this data that cost does not show up.

Shots taken with 32+ seconds left average eFG% 0.551 — ordinary, in-rhythm shots. The genuinely bad shot is the buzzer-beater: eFG% 0.347 in the last four seconds.

That reframes the decision. The 2-for-1 does not trade a good shot for a bad one. It adds a normal-quality possession, because the desperation heave at the buzzer gets taken either way. The team that holds gives up the extra possession and still takes the bad shot.

4. Possession-level upgrade: the decision-team design

The proxy limitation is closed with real possessions (pbpstats rows with start/end times via the bulk archive in public-data-availability; 1,228 of 1,230 games — python/05_possession_analysis.py also documents why a literal translation of the old design just relocates the blindness). The sharper frame: at the 40-second mark of every Q1–Q3, exactly one team has the ball — the team actually facing the 2-for-1 decision. 3,683 decision possessions, split by the observed choice:

Choice n Share Net pts from 40s Later possessions ended in TO
Quick (released ≥28s left) 3,112 84.5% +0.48 1.09 11.8%
Normal (16–27s) 547 14.9% +0.01 0.71 9.1%
Hold (carried past 15s) 24 0.7% -0.21 0.21 8.3%

Three things fall out:

  1. The debate is over in practice. Teams with the ball at the decision moment go quick 84% of the time; genuine holds are nearly extinct — 24 team-periods in a full season. The argument this study set out to test has largely been settled by the league's behaviour.
  2. The mechanism is now measured directly: quick teams average 1.09 further possessions after the mark vs 0.21 for holds — the extra possession counted, not inferred from shots.
  3. Quick minus hold on net points from the mark: +0.69 [+0.23, +1.20] — consistent with the tactic paying, but with 24 holds the comparison is honest about being underpowered. When a strategy wins the behavioural argument this thoroughly, its control group disappears.

The first-shot proxy, scored against observed choices: it flags 82% of true quicks but correctly identifies only 50% of true holds — quantifying exactly how much limitation 2 mattered.

5. What this does not establish

The treatment is not randomly assigned, and the biggest confound is possession timing: a team that does not get the ball until 20 seconds remain cannot run a 2-for-1, and is not comparable to one that had it at 40 seconds. Some of the gap is therefore selection — teams with the ball earlier were more likely to have just gotten a stop, and may be in transition against an unset defense.

The sensitivity analysis narrows that gap but does not close it. The honest statement is: teams that shoot early score more in the window, the extra possession is real and measurable, and the usual objection to the strategy — a rushed, bad shot — is not visible in the data. What limits the payoff is not shot quality but the opponent's reply: on a net basis, in the comparable sample, one season of data cannot cleanly distinguish the advantage from zero. A causal estimate would need possession-level tracking of when each team actually gained the ball, which is the natural next step.
